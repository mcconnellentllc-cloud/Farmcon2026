Competing for Land: Farmers vs. Institutional Buyers

Bill Gates owns 270,000 acres. Pension funds and private equity are pouring billions into farmland. How do you compete?

The Challenge

  • 40% of US farmland now owned by non-farmers
  • $12+ billion spent by institutions in 2021 alone
  • Institutional ownership up 10x in 15 years (now 2% of all farmland)
  • Farmland prices up 400% - massive appreciation
  • 18 million acres projected out of farming by 2035
  • 80% of farmland owned by someone 55+ (400M acres changing hands soon)

The Good News

  • 60% of sales still go to farmers, not investors
  • Only 2% of sales go to big institutions
  • "Farmers always outbid us" - Farmland Partners CEO
  • Emotional connection beats spreadsheets at auction

How Farmers Stay Competitive

  1. Build relationships with neighbors NOW - Be the first call when land comes available. Most sales never hit the open market.
  2. Keep financials strong - Low debt ratios, strong cash flow, established lender relationships let you move fast.
  3. Use every advantage - FSA loans, beginning farmer programs, seller financing - options institutions can't access.
  4. Think multi-generational - Institutions need 8-10% returns. Family farms can think in decades, not quarters.
  5. Document everything - Your yield data, soil health, and operational knowledge make YOU the best buyer for adjacent land.
  6. Consider creative structures - Lease-to-own, right of first refusal, gradual buyouts from retiring neighbors.

Watch for the Wave

400 million acres will change hands as the 55+ generation transitions. Position yourself NOW:

  • Talk to neighbors about their plans
  • Build relationship with local ag lenders
  • Explore FSA loan programs for land purchases
  • Consider forming partnerships with family members to pool resources

Sources: Halderman Real Estate, National Farmers Union

"More cash chasing poorer investments"

The current reality: There's more money looking for returns than ever before. The result?

  • Prices get bid up on mediocre assets
  • Returns get worse as prices rise
  • Institutions pile into anything that looks "investable"
  • Discipline gets replaced by desperation

Why farmland stands out:

  • Real productivity - it actually produces something
  • Inflation protection - hard asset
  • Low correlation - doesn't move with stocks

When there's too much cash chasing returns, own something real that produces something essential. That's farmland.

"Watch the spread of the amount of cash chasing an investment."

The signal: When more money floods into any asset class, returns compress. Cap rates drop. P/E ratios expand. The "spread" between what you pay and what you get shrinks.

When spreads are tight, be patient. When spreads widen (crisis, fear, distress sales) - that's when value buyers win.

Ownership Beats Inflation

  • Cash loses value - inflation erodes purchasing power
  • Wages chase inflation - always behind
  • Owners win: Land, equipment, inventory, brands appreciate
  • Debt gets cheaper - fixed payments, inflating dollars
  • Commodity prices rise - eventually

Inflation punishes savers and rewards owners.

The Big Risk: "When the world decides to stop loaning money to the US..."

The US runs on borrowed money. What happens when that stops?

  • Dollar devalues - your cash buys less
  • Interest rates spike - borrowing becomes expensive
  • Inflation explodes - everything costs more
  • Hard assets win - land, commodities, real things hold value

Farmland is the ultimate hedge: You own something real. You produce something essential. People always need to eat.

When paper wealth gets questioned, dirt doesn't.

The counterpoint: "The US is the cleanest shirt in the dirty laundry."

Yes, the US has problems. But Europe, China, Japan - they all have bigger ones. Capital still flows here because we're the least bad option. That buys time, but it's not a permanent solution.

Plan for both scenarios: the US stays strong AND prepare if it doesn't. Either way, owning productive land wins.

The Three Ownership Transition Options

Eventually, every farm owner faces this decision. Know your options:

1. Close the Business

Liquidation

  • Sell all assets - land, equipment, livestock
  • Pay off debts
  • Keep whatever remains

Worst option for legacy, but sometimes necessary. No continuation of what you built.

2. Sell the Business

Complete Sale

  • Sell to family, neighbor, or outside buyer
  • Can structure as installment sale for tax benefits
  • Operation continues under new ownership

Creates liquidity event. Legacy continues if sold to right buyer.

3. Managed Transition

Professional Management

  • Keep ownership, hire management
  • Gradual transition to next generation
  • Farm management companies handle operations

Keys to success:

  • Hire QUALIFIED operators - vet them carefully
  • Keep cash accounts accurate - trust but verify

Best of both: maintain ownership/appreciation, step back from daily operations.

Planning Ahead

  • Start 10+ years before retirement - transitions take time
  • Diversify outside the farm - don't rely 100% on sale proceeds
  • Talk to family NOW - who wants to be involved? Who doesn't?
  • Consider hybrid approaches - sell some, keep some, manage some
  • Get professional help - estate attorney, CPA, financial planner who understands ag

"The Risk is in the OPERATION of the Business"

Land appreciates. Commodities cycle. But where do farms actually FAIL?

In the operation.

  • Sloppy cash management - can't make payments when needed
  • Poor marketing - sell at the wrong time, every time
  • Uncontrolled inputs - spending without tracking ROI
  • Key person risk - everything depends on one brain
  • No systems - same mistakes, repeated yearly

Reduce operational risk by:

  • Building systems that don't depend on you remembering
  • Keeping accurate, real-time cash position
  • Documenting decisions so you learn from them
  • Training the next generation while you're still around
  • Using AI to automate the boring-but-critical stuff

The land will still be there. The question is: will your operation survive to own it?

"Population Growth is Falling Off - Peak Population is NOW"

The assumption that drove ag for decades - "more people = more food demand" - is changing.

  • Global fertility rates dropping faster than predicted
  • Peak population may arrive sooner than 2100 projections
  • Developed nations already shrinking (Japan, Europe, soon US)
  • China's population now declining

What this means for ag:

  • Total demand growth slows - can't count on population to bail you out
  • Quality over quantity - feeding fewer, wealthier people who want better food
  • Protein demand shifts - rising incomes = more meat consumption (for now)
  • Export markets change - who are you selling to in 20 years?

The world isn't running out of food. It may be running out of customers who need more of it. Differentiation matters more than ever.

"How to Create Enterprise Value"

Billion dollar contracts flow through the ag industry. The question: how much of that value can YOU capture?

Enterprise Value = Asset Value + Business Value

  • Asset Value: Land, equipment, inventory - what you could liquidate
  • Business Value: Relationships, contracts, systems, brand, cash flow - what makes it MORE than just assets

Most farms sell for asset value only. Why? Because there's no business to sell - just land and iron.

How to build enterprise value:

  • Contracts - locked-in buyers, premium programs, forward contracts
  • Relationships - landlords, suppliers, buyers who prefer YOU
  • Systems - documented processes that run without you
  • Brand - reputation, certifications, direct customer relationships
  • Cash flow - proven, consistent, documented profitability
  • Data - yield history, soil maps, operational records

A farm with $2M in assets and $200K proven cash flow is worth more than a farm with $2M in assets and "it depends on the year."

Alternative Revenue: Solar Panels

  • Dual-use: agrivoltaics allow farming under/around panels
  • Carbon/ESG value for land portfolio

Every dollar of contracted, predictable income adds to enterprise value.
